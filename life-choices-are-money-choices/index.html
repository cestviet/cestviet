Life choices are money choices

Everyone pitied my S$5,000 "local contract." Nobody ran the math.
 
Picture this: early 2010, France. You're graduating top of your class with a master's in finance and banking. Big-name banks are already sliding offers across the table.
 
The catch: a six-month countdown for a work permit — and nobody can promise you'll get it.
 
You've spent seven years building a life here from zero. The language. The friends. The belonging.
 
Now, the machine you were up against. None of it was personal — and that was the problem.
 
The market. Wall Street broke in 2008; French hiring broke in 2009. Unemployment jumped from 7.4% to 9.1% in a year. For under-25s: roughly one in four. And of the young people who did have jobs, about half were on temporary contracts. Translation: companies weren't hiring people. They were renting them.
 
The clock. The 2006 immigration law — its philosophy was literally called immigration choisie, "selective immigration" — gave non-EU master's graduates exactly one concession: six months to find a qualifying job. Non-renewable. A countdown, not a safety net. Bachelor's grads didn't even get the countdown.
 
The fine print. The job had to match your degree and pay at least 1.5× the minimum wage — about €2,000 gross a month in 2010. Miss either box, and your employer had to prove no one already on the French labor market could do your job. Try proving a labor shortage in a year when a quarter of your generation was jobless.
 
The mood. This was the era of a Ministry of Immigration, Integration and National Identity, annual deportation targets, and a state-sponsored "Great Debate on National Identity." No one had to ban you. The message lived in the paperwork: months-long queues, discretion at every counter, and employers who needed someone who could start Monday.
On paper, a top-of-class master's grad with bank offers was exactly the immigrant this system claimed to want. On paper.
 
So what do you do? Follow your heart and bet a decade of your life on a maybe? Or follow your head, pack up, and go somewhere that actually wants you?
 
Here's what nobody tells you — and school never teaches: this was never just a heart-vs-head decision. It was a long-term money question in disguise.
Plot twist: that wasn't a hypothetical. That was me — a Vietnamese grad, seven years into a French life built from zero. And I left.
 
One-way ticket to Singapore. A "local contract" with regional scope at a U.S. tech giant: S$5,000 a month. No expat housing. No relocation package. Seven years of belonging, packed into checked luggage. People back in France gave me that polite, tilted-head sympathy.
 
Everyone pitied the local contract. Nobody ran the math.
Here it is on a napkin. Rough numbers — but they're my numbers.
 
The lottery ticket vs. the sure thing. A French bank offer paid about €40,000 a year — if a prefecture let me take it. Six months of waiting first: no salary, about €1,000 a month of Paris rent and food, and a clerk with discretion at the end of it. A job offer you might not be allowed to accept isn't an offer. It's a lottery ticket with a rent bill. Singapore? A salary comfortably above the Employment Pass bar, paperwork measured in days, and a start date.
 
The haircut. In France, roughly 30 cents of every euro I earned vanished before I paid rent — social charges, then income tax. In Singapore, about 5. Same person, same work, radically different haircut. And yes — France's 30% bought a pension and healthcare. Singapore's 5% meant the safety net was mine to build. Fine. I'd rather build it myself with the other 25 cents. Singapore also didn't tax investment gains. France did.
 
Do the six-month math: start in Singapore, save roughly €9,000 after rent and food. Wait in Paris, burn roughly €6,000. That coin flip cost about €15,000 — win or lose. And I was saving in a currency that gained about 14% against the euro that year alone.
 
The refund policy. Here's the part nobody tells you. The Singapore move was reversible: if it failed, I'd still have the French master's, a Fortune 500 line on my CV, and savings in a hard currency. The Paris wait was the irreversible one. The permit was non-renewable. So is your time.
 
The price tag. In France, I was one of thousands of identical graduates with a visa problem. You don't set your price in a market oversupplied with you. In Singapore, a finance grad with Vietnamese and French, covering APAC for an American tech company, was a scarcity. Your market value isn't a personality trait. It's a location.
 
And the scariest line item wasn't financial. My English was weak. Regional calls, in my third language, with executives who didn't slow down for anyone. Seven years of classrooms hadn't done what twelve months of those calls did. Vietnamese, French, English — three languages, three markets. And Saigon was suddenly a two-hour flight away instead of twelve.
 
The verdict. France's unemployment didn't peak in 2010. It kept climbing until 2015, to around 10%. I didn't abandon the ship at the worst moment. I left five years before it stopped sinking.
 
The lesson. You're not choosing between Paris and Singapore. But you're choosing something: the "safe" job in a shrinking industry. The city that eats half your salary. The path you're staying on because of the years already spent.
 
Run the napkin math. Not because money is everything — because money is the part everyone politely refuses to count.
 
Every life-changing decision is a long-term money decision. And the worst markets hide the best entry prices.
 
Resilience isn't proving you can survive a bad market. It's refusing to donate your best years to one. Grit without math is just expensive stubbornness.

2. Execution risk: a conditional offer is not an asset

France. Under CESEDA art. L.311-11 (2006 framework), a master's graduate with a job related to the degree paying ≥1.5× SMIC was exempt from the labor market test (opposabilité de la situation de l'emploi). At ~€2,006/month gross (SMIC €8.82/h × 151.67h × 1.5, July 2009), the €40k offer cleared it. The binding constraints were not the test. They were: a six-month, non-renewable job-search permit; prefecture processing measured in months; employer start-date intolerance; and a discretionary rejection tail.
 
Singapore. S$5,000/month cleared the P2 Employment Pass qualifying salary in force in 2010 — S$3,500, raised to S$4,000 in December 2011. No labor market test applied to EP holders in 2010. Processing: days.

Year 1 extension (assumption-dependent): if the French permit lands at month 6, the French path saves ~€1,700 for the year against Singapore's ~€18,300 — a ~€16,500 gap.

5. The real arbitrage is the savings rate

Singapore gross was lower in euro terms (€33,300 vs €40,000). Yet:
 
  • Singapore: ~€1,530/mo saved ≈ 55% of gross
  • Paris (once working): ~€1,290/mo saved ≈ 39% of gross
 
Note: EUR/SGD moved from ~2.00 (Jan 2010) to ~1.72 (Dec 2010) — roughly 14% euro depreciation. This is a euro-denominated valuation gain on SGD savings, not a rise in Singapore purchasing power. MAS's basket-band-crawl framework leaned toward appreciation post-2009. Untaxed gains on invested savings complete the effect.

6. Reversibility and positioning

Real option. Singapore was reversible: failure after 12–18 months still left the French master's, a Fortune 500 line on the CV, and hard-currency savings. The wait in France was not: six months of prime earning years, non-refundable, against a non-renewable permit.
 
Positioning. France: an oversupplied market of comparable graduates plus visa friction — a price-taker. Singapore: a talent-scarce regional hub, with APAC P&L exposure rather than a siloed domestic desk. Language was an operational ramp-up cost, not a structural barrier; twelve months of regional calls produced working trilingual capability (lived experience, not a statistic). Proximity: Saigon ~2h, Hanoi ~3.5h from Singapore; Paris–Vietnam ~12h.

7. Macro backdrop

Singapore 2010 real GDP growth ≈14.5–15% (MTI/SingStat). France ≈2% (INSEE), with the eurozone sovereign debt crisis unfolding. French unemployment did not peak in 2010 — it climbed to ≈10% by 2015 (INSEE, series-dependent). The exit was five years early, not late.

8. What would falsify this thesis

This is not a claim that Singapore beats France. It is a claim about 2010 conditions. The case collapses if: the French permit had been fast and near-certain; the Singapore role had no regional scope; the French welfare bundle were valued above ~25% of gross; the SGD had depreciated; or the operator could not function in English.

The point is not to romanticise migration. It is to stop romanticising sunk costs. Your time is capital. Spend it where odds, cash flow, and exit options improve — not where nostalgia feels safest. 

Life choices are money choices. Count them.

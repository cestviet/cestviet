Life choices are money choices (II)

Vietnamien et fier de l'être Monday, 16 November 2026

PART ONE — THE DECISION

 
S$180. That was the fare, Changi to Tân Sơn Nhất and back. I paid it twenty-six times in six months.
 
Monday to Friday I ran a regional portfolio for a U.S. technology group — business laptops and IT solutions into large corporates across Asia-Pacific. Saturday morning I was in a hospital corridor in Saigon. Sunday night I was back at Changi, ironing a shirt for Monday. I did not have a weekend for half a year. I didn't notice, because there was nothing else to notice.
 
The flights came to S$4,680. I know that number because I have always known my numbers. What I didn't know until it was over was the other one: a hundred thousand US dollars. Everything I had saved in France. Everything I had saved in Singapore. The treatment. Roughly two years of my entire Singapore net income. About fifty-seven times what the average Vietnamese earned in a year in 2012.
 
In health economics there is a term for this — catastrophic health expenditure. It is not a metaphor. It is a threshold, and I was multiple past it. In Vietnam that year the insurer of last resort was not a policy. It was your family's own balance sheet. Out-of-pocket spending sat near 40% of total health expenditure. Coverage has widened a great deal since. But in that corridor, in that year, the policy was me.
 
Mom died. Then I moved home.
 
Everyone assumes the first caused the second. It didn't. I had already decided to return, and for a reason that has nothing to do with grief.
That year I sat in a bidding room quoting a Japanese retail conglomerate, group-wide, and a Vietnamese telecom group that was climbing. The Vietnamese order came in at three times the Japanese one — laptops, licensed software, accessories, warranty. In the slowest growth year this country had seen since 1999, a domestic group was building a salaried workforce at three times the scale of a Japanese blue chip.
 
Licensed software meant they were paying real money to formalize. A warranty package meant someone in that building was costing total ownership — which meant a finance function with actual authority. Salaried headcount is the leading indicator of the modern-trade basket, with a lag of a couple of years. I was reading the Vietnamese consumer of 2015 in a 2012 procurement document. No dashboard in Singapore was going to tell me that for two more years.
 
I remember thinking: this is the place to be. Not eventually. Now.
 
Then three multinationals bid for me at once, and I understood something else — that whatever the market thought of Vietnam, it did not think there were many of me. I had six headhunters approaching at the Finance Manager and Deputy Director level. Three offers closed. Four conversations were still open when I signed. In Singapore that same year I was a Senior Financial Analyst, and my own plan had me reaching, potentially, Manager in 2016. Vietnam offered me the grade four years early. Not because I was better there. Because there were fewer of me.
 
People ask why I took the lowest offer of the three. I didn't. I took the one with the most in it.
 
The three offers were not three salaries. They were three bets with different shapes.
 
The U.S. semiconductor group paid the most and told me the least about this country. A chip assembly plant is a cost centre inside an export bubble — you report variance and transfer-pricing and learn almost nothing about what a Vietnamese family does with its first pay rise. The U.S. consumer-goods giant paid well and handed me somebody else's playbook. The French retail distribution group paid the least and asked me to build the function from nothing — financial control from purchase centre to site, retail margin and rental roll — in a market that did not yet have the job title.
 
It was the steepest learning curve available, the fastest way to read a middle class being born in front of us, and the one position where whatever I built would have my name on it.
 
I did not take a pay cut. I bought the cheapest entry into the highest-upside position in the market. That is not generosity to Vietnam. That is an investment.
 
It was assessed the way I would assess any capital deployment. Downside: I had no savings, so a failure would hurt. Upside: large and skewed — a built function, a name, a career that compounds instead of one that rents. And the alternative's risk was the one nobody prices: a strong base on a commoditized path in a market where I was abundant. That risk arrives as a four per cent review and an unchanged title, and looks like safety for fourteen years. I took the sharp risk over the dull one. Sharp risks you can see and survive. Dull ones you cannot.
 
But the investment only worked because the alternative was structurally closed.
 
The woman I wanted to marry looked for work in Singapore. She did not find any. Not because she was not capable, but because she needed an Employment Pass she could not get, in a market the authorities had just tightened. As my girlfriend she had no dependant route. Staying meant one salary. And one salary in Singapore, for the life we wanted, did not close.
 
In Saigon she needed no pass. Her savings went into the down payment alongside mine — five hundred million đồng, two people, one flat, ninety square metres, a pool, a riverside park.
 
I did not come home for the salary. I came home because Singapore could not hold the life I was trying to build, and Vietnam could hold it on one income.
 
Fourteen years later I am a CFO. The initial scarcity premium — twenty-four per cent, then twenty-two, then twenty, tapering down to fifteen — was the market manufacturing people like me. The curve did not flatten because I did not sit on that advantage. I moved from retail into five further industries, each time stepping into a mandate the local market could not yet fill, each time resetting the scarcity clock before the previous premium finished eroding. Scarcity is not something you own. It is something you have to keep earning.
 
I might have been wrong. I bought the flat in 2015, before the property run, and I will not pretend that timing was entirely skill. The consumer-goods giant at thirty-five million was probably the better line on my CV for five years. Plenty who came home in 2012 with better papers got stuck. I am a survivor of this trade describing it, not a proof of it.
 
Mom never saw the flat. That is the one line I cannot make balance.
 

PART TWO — THE AUDIT TRAIL

What follows is the financial model underlying the 2012 decision, executed January 2013 and lived through to 2026. Every cell is sourced. Nothing is projected from memory.

The semiconductor group's two-month sign-on put it effectively on par with the consumer-goods giant in Year 1. From Year 2 it fell to 24.78m — under two million above the retail group. The real net gap I accepted against the consumer-goods offer was roughly 6m a month.

2. Scarcity decay versus the realised CAGR

This is the most important finding in the model — and the one that separates an audit from a victory lap.
 
The taper (scarcity decay). The retail group's offer implied an initial raise trajectory of ~24%, decaying year by year to ~15%. That decay is the sound of the market manufacturing substitutes for the 2012 returnee profile.
 
The realised CAGR. From VND 27.5m in February 2013 to VND 171m in 2026 is a multiple of 6.22x — a 15.1% compound annual rate. This is not a single-firm ladder. It is the market repricing my capability across a French retail network and then five further firms in five further industries.
 
The inference. A decaying initial premium cannot produce a sustained 15% compound over thirteen years inside one role. The realised CAGR proves the initial advantage was replaced by successive scarcity positions — each move into a sector the local market could not yet supply leadership for, resetting the scarcity clock before the previous premium finished eroding. Scarcity here is not inherited. It is re-earned at every border crossed.

3. The housing ledger — the structural wall

This is where the Singapore counterfactual mathematically fails for a mono-income household.
 
Vietnam (actual). Purchased a 90 sqm riverside condominium in 2015 for VND 1.5 billion. VND 500m joint down payment; VND 1b mortgage on a ten-year tenor; settled early in 2020 through aggressive saving, with ~VND 400m total interest paid over sixty months. Total cash deployed: VND 1.9 billion.Current market value: VND 3.5 billion. Imputed market rent VND 15m/month — a gross yield of 5.14%— debt-free.
 
Singapore (counterfactual). To replicate a 90 sqm, two-bedroom private condominium with full facilities requires an asset of roughly S$1.8m–2.0m. As a foreign national on an Employment Pass, the Additional Buyer's Stamp Duty applies. Introduced at 10% for foreigners in December 2011, it rose to 15% in January 2013, 20% in 2018, 30% in 2021, and 60% in April 2023. At the current rate, the stamp duty alone on a S$1.9m property is ~S$1.14 million — a pure dead-weight loss paid before a single brick is owned. Even at the 2013 rate of 15%, the dead-weight loss was ~S$285,000 — prohibitive for a mono-income household struggling to close a monthly basket. The Singapore path rents forever. The Vietnam path converts cash into a yielding, debt-free asset.

4. The family basket multiple (2026 endpoint)

We do not compare salaries at nominal FX. We compare net income against the cost of an equivalent comfortable family life — spouse, one secondary-school child.
 
Vietnam (actual, 2026). Gross VND 171m/month plus a 2.5-month average bonus gives ~VND 2,479m annual gross. Net monthly income, averaged with current dependent deductions: ~VND 147.6m. The all-in household basket is VND 60m ongoing plus ~VND 11m of averaged lumpy costs — gifts to both families, hiếu hỉ, overseas travel, home improvements — totalling VND 71m. Add VND 15m imputed rent on the owned flat for a full economic cost of VND 86m.
Basket multiple: 147.6 ÷ 86 = 1.72x. Net income buys 1.7 times the full economic family standard.
 
Singapore (market-anchored counterfactual, 2026). Associate Director, S$15,000 base plus a two-month bonus = S$210,000 annual gross. Net after resident tax: ~S$15,595. Equivalent family basket — S$5,000 rent for the comparable condominium, S$3,000 secondary school, S$7,000 food, transport, household, insurance, travel — totalling S$15,000.
Basket multiple: 15,595 ÷ 15,000 = 1.04x. On one income at the equivalent standard, the Associate Director barely covers the basket. No capital left to buy the home. No buffer for a shock.
 
(A cheaper Singapore life exists — public housing, local school, a basket near S$10,500 — but that is a different life, not a cheaper version of the same one. We hold the standard constant. A stalled-path branch, Senior Financial Analyst at 4% throughout with no promotion, ends at ~S$8,658/month in 2026.)

5. The reconciliation and the relational ledger

Running a smooth 15% interpolation against a 10m→60m living-cost curve yields a modelled cumulative cash surplus of roughly VND 5.6 billion. The reported cash position in 2026 is ~US$150,000 (≈ VND 3.9 billion).
 
The ~VND 1.7 billion gap is not an error. It is the relational ledger — roughly VND 11m a month over thirteen years deployed into the Vietnamese family structure: supporting parents, wedding envelopes, funeral envelopes, the unquantifiable cost of holding a family network together. This is the Vietnamese household's actual insurance premium. It does not appear in any international cost-of-living table, but it is the most durable line item on the balance sheet.

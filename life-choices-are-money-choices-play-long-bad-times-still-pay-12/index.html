Life choices are money choices. Play long — bad times still pay! 1/2

Vietnamien et fier de l'être Friday, 13 November 2026
Picture this: early 2010, France. You're graduating top of your class with a master's in finance and banking. Big-name banks are already sliding offers across the table.
 
The catch: a six-month countdown for a work permit — and nobody can promise you'll get it.
 
You've spent ten years building a life here from zero. The language. The friends. The belonging.
 
Now, you were up against the system. None of it was personal — and that was the problem.
 
The market. Wall Street broke in 2008; French hiring broke in 2009. Unemployment jumped from 7.4% to 9.1% in a year. For under-25s: roughly one in four. And of the young people who did have jobs, about half were on temporary contracts. Translation: companies weren't hiring people. They were renting them.
 
The clock. The 2006 immigration law — its philosophy was literally called immigration choisie, "selective immigration" — gave non-EU master's graduates exactly one concession: six months to find a qualifying job. Non-renewable. A countdown, not a safety net.
 
The fine print. The job had to match your degree and pay at least 1.5× the minimum wage — about €2,000 gross a month in 2010. And your employer had to prove no one already on the French labor market could do your job. Try proving a labor shortage in a year when a quarter of your generation was jobless!
 
The mood. This was the era of a Ministry of Immigration, Integration and National Identity, annual deportation targets, and a state-sponsored "Great Debate on National Identity." No one had to ban you. The message lived in the paperwork: months-long queues, discretion at every step, and employers who needed someone who could start Monday.
 
On paper, a top-of-class master's grad with bank offers was exactly the immigrant this system claimed to want. On paper.
 
So what do you do? Follow your heart and bet a decade of your life on a maybe? Or follow your head, pack up, and go somewhere that actually wants you?
 
Here's what nobody tells you — and school never teaches: this was never just a heart-vs-head decision. It was a long-term money question in disguise.

1. The market

  • Unemployment 7.4% (2008) → 9.1% (2009) → 9.3% (2010): INSEE, Enquête Emploi, ILO/BIT unemployment rate, France métropolitaine, annual averages. → insee.fr (series: "taux de chômage au sens du BIT"). Cross-check in INSEE, Tableaux de l'économie française, 2010 and 2011 editions. 
  • Youth (15–24) unemployment ≈ 1 in 4 in 2009 (≈19% in 2008): same INSEE series, age breakdown; Eurostat annual youth unemployment (dataset une_rt_a) as cross-check. 
  • ~Half of working youth on temporary contracts (48.8% in 2009, vs ~9% for 25–49): DG Trésor, Trésor-Éco n° 92, "Le chômage des jeunes : quel diagnostic ?" (2011). → tresor.economie.gouv.fr, Trésor-Éco archive. Alternative source: DARES analyses on short contracts, 2010–2011 (dares.travail-emploi.gouv.fr).
  • The lag: the financial shock hit in 2007–2008; French employment contracted through 2009. INSEE national accounts / L'économie française, édition 2010. (2009 GDP fell roughly 3%)

2. The clock

  • Six-month, non-renewable post-study permit (APS), master's-level only: CESEDA, art. L.311-11, as created by loi n° 2006-911 du 24 juillet 2006 relative à l'immigration et à l'intégration ("Sarkozy II"). → legifrance.gouv.fr, use "version en vigueur" for 2009–2010. Licence (Bac+3) graduates were excluded from the APS in this period; the extension to licence professionnelle came later (loi 2016-274).
  • "Immigration choisie": the stated philosophy of the 2006 law — the term is in the legislative record itself (Assemblée nationale / Sénat dossiers on the 2006 bill). → assemblee-nationale.fr, senat.fr.

3. The fine print

  • Exemption from the labor market test for APS holders: job related to the degree and paid ≥ 1.5× SMIC — this is the honest version of the story. Miss either condition and you fell back to the general rule. → CESEDA L.311-11 (2009–2010 version) + implementing texts. 
  • The general rule ("opposabilité de la situation de l'emploi"): employer had to show no suitable candidate already on the French labor market — vacancy posted with Pôle emploi (which replaced ANPE in January 2009). → Code du travail, art. L.5221-2 and the R.5221-* regulatory articles, 2009–2010 version.
  • The €2,000/month figure, shown as arithmetic: SMIC horaire brut = €8.82 (July 2009), €8.86 (Jan 2010) → monthly 35h ≈ €1,338 / €1,344 → ×1.5 ≈ €2,006 / €2,016 gross. → INSEE SMIC series; JORF SMIC decrees.
  • Short contracts → short cards: CDDs mapped to the travailleur temporaire card (CESEDA L.313-10), duration tied to the contract — precarity, not a statutory "12-month minimum."

4. The mood

  • Ministry of Immigration, Integration, National Identity and Co-development: Éric Besson, January 2009 – November 2010. → JORF government composition decrees, legifrance.gouv.fr.
  • Annual removal targets: budget performance indicators, mission "Immigration, asile et intégration" (programme 303), PLF 2009–2010; Senate budget reports. 
  • Grand débat sur l'identité nationale: launched 2 November 2009. → contemporary press coverage (Le Monde, Libération, Nov 2009–Jan 2010); ministry archives via BnF web archive.
  • Processing times and prefecture discretion: sourced as lived experience — say so. For contemporaneous documentation of conversion refusals, see La Cimade and GISTI publications from 2009–2011 (cimade.org, gisti.org).

5. And it got worse before it got better

  • Circulaire Guéant, 31 May 2011, tightening student-to-worker conversions — after our window, which is exactly the point. Full text republished by GISTI (gisti.org).
  • Loi n° 2011-672 du 16 juin 2011 ("loi Besson"): draft adopted in Council of Ministers 31 March 2010. → legifrance.gouv.fr; vie-publique.fr dossier.
  • Unemployment kept climbing to ≈10.4% in 2015 (INSEE BIT series).
